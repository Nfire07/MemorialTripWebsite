Exhibition “Lili Jacob Album”

Also known as the “Auschwitz Album”, it is a set of photographs taken in 1944 on the arrival ramp of Auschwitz-Birkenau during the convoys, particularly from Hungary, documenting with stark clarity arrival, selection, and the first stages of dispossession. The album was found in 1945 by the young deportee Lili Jacob in a barrack of a camp where she had been transferred. She understood the historical value of those images and preserved them, allowing historians, courts, and witnesses to recognise faces, places, and procedures. Today it is one of the most frequently cited visual collections for understanding “how” the machinery of extermination functioned, not only “what” happened.

Historical context

The photographs were taken by members of the SS photographic service at Birkenau in the spring–summer of 1944, during the mass deportations of Hungarian Jews. The sequences show trains arriving, prisoners lining up, checks, the separation between those “fit for work” and those sent directly to the crematoria. The strength of the album lies in its sequential nature: these are not isolated shots, but a coherent and deliberately organised visual account of violence.

The discovery by Lili Jacob in 1945, shortly after liberation, turned these images into primary evidence: thanks to the album many families were able to reconstruct journeys and recognise people and settings. In the post-war period, copies and reproductions were used in trials and exhibitions, contributing to the public memory of the Shoah. The controlled circulation of the album has also fostered comparative studies on locations, uniforms, and procedures.

What we learn

  • Photography as evidence: understanding the evidential value of images and their limits (partial framing, the perpetrator’s point of view, the absence of what lies “beyond” the ramp), while questioning who is taking the picture and why.
  • Vocabulary of deportation: arrivals, selections, registrations, confiscation of personal belongings, loss of name and civil identity, connected to orders, paperwork, and the logistics of the convoys.
  • Sequence and causal links: reading the photos not as single “frames”, but as stages of an administrative–industrial process of extermination, linking gestures and spaces to decisions and responsibilities.

What to observe (class focus)

  • Composition and details: signs, uniforms, personal objects, looks, gestures: which “minor” pieces of information open up bigger questions?
  • The ramp as a space: where exactly are we? how are the flows organised? who directs and who waits?
  • Causes and effects: connect the photographic sequence to regulations, orders, and convoy logistics (who decides what, when, and why).
